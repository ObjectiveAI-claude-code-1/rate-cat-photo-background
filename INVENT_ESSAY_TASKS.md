1. Assess visual subordination by examining the background for any individual elements that rival or outrank the cat in visual prominence. Look past the cat and identify anything in the surrounding environment that is brighter, sharper, or more visually striking than the cat itself — a glowing screen, a brightly colored object, another animal, a person, vivid signage, or any element that surges forward and demands the viewer's attention. A subordinate background does not require emptiness; a detailed garden in soft bokeh or a furnished room with muted tones can be fully subordinate. What matters is the relationship of prominence: every element in the background should accept a quieter rank than the cat, allowing the cat to stand as the most visually commanding presence in the frame. Score highly when nothing in the background challenges the cat's position at the top of the visual hierarchy, and score poorly when one or more elements break that hierarchy by competing for the viewer's eye.

2. Assess attentional cohesion by evaluating whether the background as a whole scatters the viewer's gaze across the frame, even when no single element dominates. Where the first task looks for individual competitors, this task examines the collective effect of everything behind the cat. Look for visual clutter — accumulations of small objects on surfaces, busy or repeating patterns like checkered floors or wildly patterned rugs, densely packed shelves full of varied items — anything that creates a field of visual noise the eye feels compelled to inventory. A background succeeds at cohesion when it presents the viewer with nothing to catalog, when soft textures, uniform surfaces, muted tones, or gently blurred elements allow the gaze to land on the cat and rest there without being pulled in multiple directions. Score highly when the background feels like atmosphere rather than inventory, and score poorly when the cumulative busyness of the environment fragments attention and makes the cat feel like one object among many.

3. Assess tonal harmony by evaluating whether the colors, brightness, and contrast across the background exist in peaceful relationship with the cat and with each other. Look for tonal disruptions that fracture the visual calm of the image — harsh shadows cutting across surfaces, windows or light sources blown out to pure white against darker surroundings, objects in clashing or aggressively saturated colors that jar against the prevailing palette, or abrupt transitions in brightness that create hard edges the eye is involuntarily drawn to. Also consider the tonal relationship between the background and the cat itself: the background should provide enough contrast for the cat to be distinct without so much contrast that the separation feels jarring, and it should not be so tonally similar that the cat merges into its surroundings and loses definition. Score highly when the entire frame feels tonally unified — colors complementing each other, light falling evenly or graduating softly, the cat sitting naturally within a cohesive visual world — and score poorly when tonal clashes, harsh lighting, or insufficient separation between cat and background disrupt the visual peace of the photograph.
